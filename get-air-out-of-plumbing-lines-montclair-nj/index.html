How to Get Air Out of Plumbing Lines in Montclair, NJ

Call (973) 488-5410

By the Montclair Express Plumbing team. Published November 12, 2026. Updated November 12, 2026.

Air in plumbing lines makes faucets sputter, spit water in bursts and sometimes bang pipes when you shut a tap. It usually follows work on the system, such as a water heater swap, a repair or a water shutoff in the street. Most of the time, you can clear it yourself in a few minutes.

If the air returns, the cause may be a leak or a failing part, and that is when to call. Dispatch at (973) 488-5410 can set up a visit.

How to bleed the lines

The goal is to push the air out through a high point while the system refills. Start with the water supply on and work from the lowest tap to the highest, so the air has a path to leave.

  1. Turn the water supply on slowly. Open the main valve and let the lines fill.
  2. Open the lowest faucet. Let it run until the flow is steady.
  3. Open faucets upward. Work through each floor, ending with the highest tap in the house.
  4. Run hot and cold. Open both sides at each tap until the spitting stops.

Do not forget the water heater

Air also collects in the heater, and hot water taps may sputter after the heater has been drained. Run the hot taps until the flow is steady before you turn the heater back on. An electric tank must be full before power is restored, or the elements can burn out.

If you drain and refill a tank yourself, close the drain valve first and open a hot tap while the tank fills. If the heater is leaking or behaving oddly, see water heater repair.

When trapped air means something is wrong

These signs suggest a leak, a failing valve or a damaged section of pipe. A plumber can test pressure and trace it.

  • Air returns every few days.
  • Pressure drops along with the sputtering.
  • You notice damp spots or a rising water bill.
  • Pipes bang even when nothing has been worked on.

Older homes in Montclair

Many Montclair houses have long runs of older supply pipe, and galvanized lines hold air and scale in places that make sputtering more frequent. A pattern of sputtering and falling pressure together is often a sign of aging supply pipe. If the problem matches, see repiping for what that involves.

Banging pipes and water hammer

A bang when a tap or washing machine valve closes is water hammer. The moving water stops suddenly and the pipe slams against its supports. It is not always the same as air, though air chambers that have filled with water can make it worse.

Securing loose pipes, installing hammer arrestors and checking pressure can solve it. If your water pressure is high, a pressure reducing valve protects fixtures as well as quiets the pipes.

Pressure problems that look like air

Low pressure and sputtering sometimes come from a partly closed valve, a clogged aerator or a failing pressure regulator. If only one faucet sputters, remove and clean its aerator. If the whole house is affected, check the main valve and the meter valve to be sure they are fully open.

A pattern that persists after you have bled the lines and checked valves needs a plumber. We can test pressure, listen for leaks and tell you what is happening.

After plumbing work or a shutoff

Air gets into the lines whenever the water is turned off, a pipe is cut or the city shuts a main for repairs. When the water returns, open the faucet farthest from the entry point last so air has a place to leave, and run the lowest taps first. Expect sputtering for a minute or two.

Remove aerators for the first run so grit does not clog them, then flush each tap with cold water before using hot. Check the water heater, because air in the tank can make it knock for a day.

When air points to a bigger problem

Air that keeps returning after you bleed the lines can mean a leak on the supply side, a failing well or city pressure fault, or a defective water heater. A pipe that sucks air in is also a pipe that can leak water out. Mention how often it happens when you call.

Codes and standards behind the work

Water hammer arrestors are made to ASSE 1010, the standard that sizes them for fixture loads, and a pressure reducing valve is used where street pressure runs high.
