Do You Need a Water Softener in Montclair, NJ?

Call (973) 488-5410

By the Montclair Express Plumbing team. Published November 12, 2026. Updated November 12, 2026.

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. When it is heated or evaporates, those minerals leave a chalky scale on faucets, shower heads and inside water heaters. Whether you need a softener depends on how hard your water is and how much the scale bothers you.

You do not have to guess. Montclair publishes an annual water quality report, and it lists hardness. Read it, compare it with the signs in your house and then decide. Call (973) 488-5410 if you want a second opinion.

Signs of hard water

  • White or gray crust on faucets and shower heads.
  • Spots on glasses and dishes after the dishwasher runs.
  • Soap that does not lather well.
  • A water heater that rumbles and loses capacity over time.
  • Appliances and fixtures that wear sooner than expected.

Reading your water report

Hardness is usually reported in parts per million or grains per gallon. Higher numbers mean harder water. The report also lists the sources of your supply, which helps explain why hardness differs from town to town.

If the report lists a number you do not understand, call us. We can explain it in plain terms and tell you what it means for your water heater and fixtures.

Softener, conditioner or nothing

A softener is not a filter. It does not remove chlorine, lead or other contaminants, so a different treatment is needed for those concerns.

What hard water does to a water heater

Scale insulates the bottom of a tank and the elements inside it. Gas tanks work harder to heat through the scale, and electric elements burn out sooner. Tankless units are more sensitive and need descaling on a schedule.

If you are choosing a new heater, hardness is part of the decision. See tankless water heater for how we handle scale on those units.

Montclair water and what to read

The township report is the primary source for what is in your water, so use it instead of general claims. Glen Ridge publishes its own report, so use the one for your town. New Jersey now requires water systems to replace lead service lines, so if your report discusses lead, ask what material runs from the street to your meter.

How a salt based softener works

Hard water passes through a tank of resin beads that hold sodium ions. As the water flows past, calcium and magnesium stick to the beads and sodium goes into the water. When the beads are full, the unit regenerates with a salt brine and flushes the minerals down a drain.

That regeneration needs a drain, a power outlet and a supply of salt. Placement matters, because the drain connection and the line to the water heater must be planned. A plumber can set this up and check your water pressure at the same time.

What to weigh before you buy

A softener solves scale. It is a decision about convenience and the life of your appliances, not an emergency. Take your time and read the numbers.

  • The hardness number in your water report.
  • Space for the tank and a salt supply.
  • A drain and an outlet nearby.
  • Whether softened water suits your household and any health advice you follow.
  • Ongoing cost of salt and maintenance.

Alternatives to a full softener

A whole house softener is not the only answer. Descaling cartridges on a single appliance, a showerhead filter or simply flushing a water heater once a year each deal with part of the problem at lower cost. They do not remove hardness the way a softener does, but they can slow scale where it hurts most.

Match the tool to the complaint. If spots on glasses bother you, a dishwasher rinse aid helps. If the water heater is loud and slow, flushing it does more than any filter.

Signs that scale is costing you money

White crust on faucets, cloudy glassware and a water heater that rumbles are all signs of mineral buildup. Over time, scale cuts heater efficiency and shortens appliance life. Fixing it costs far less than replacing a fixture early.

Codes and standards behind the work

Hardness is reported in grains per gallon or milligrams per liter, and the U.S. Geological Survey scale treats water above about 7 grains per gallon as hard. Your water supplier publishes its own report.
