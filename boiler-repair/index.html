Why a boiler stops heating

Boilers rely on water, fuel, a spark or pilot and a circulating pump. A fault in any of them stops the heat. The most common causes are low water pressure, a failed circulator pump, a faulty thermostat or an igniter that no longer lights.

Check the pressure gauge before you call. If it reads near zero, the system has lost water, which suggests a leak. Do not add water without guidance because a hot boiler can be damaged by cold water.

Boilers are serviceable for many years, and good maintenance extends that. Age matters, and we are honest when replacement is wiser than repair.

Boilers in older Montclair homes

Many Montclair houses built before the middle of the last century were designed around a boiler and radiators. The boiler may have been replaced, though the piping and radiators can be original. Older systems have longer pipe runs and more places where air or sediment can collect.

This is why diagnosis matters. A radiator that stays cold may have air in it, or the system may have a balance problem. We check the whole loop before replacing parts.

Regular maintenance

An annual service finds worn parts before a cold night does. Combustion checks, pressure checks and a look at the venting protect you from carbon monoxide and from emergency calls.

  • Check pressure and the relief valve.
  • Inspect the circulator pump and expansion tank.
  • Clean and test ignition and burner.
  • Check venting and combustion air.
  • Bleed radiators and look for leaks.

Safety and carbon monoxide

Fuel burning appliances can produce carbon monoxide when they are not venting or burning properly. Working detectors on every floor are essential, and a boiler with soot, a yellow flame or a smell of gas needs a technician right away. If a detector sounds, leave the house and call 911.

We check combustion and venting as part of a boiler repair, and we tell you what we find, even when it is not what you called about.

Codes and standards behind the work

Gas fired boilers fall under the National Fuel Gas Code (NFPA 54), which covers piping, venting and combustion air, and permit rules in New Jersey are set by the local construction office.
