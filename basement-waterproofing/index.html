Where basement water comes from

Basement water enters in a few predictable ways. Groundwater rises and pushes through the floor and wall joints. Surface water pools against the foundation and seeps in through cracks. Plumbing failures, such as a leaking pipe or a sewer backup, can mimic these.

We look for the source before we recommend a fix. A basement with a leaking supply line and one with high groundwater need different solutions.

Montclair homes and wet basements

Many Montclair homes are built on hillsides or on lots that slope toward the foundation. Basements in older houses often have fieldstone or block walls and original floors without drains. After Hurricane Ida in September 2021, many homeowners across northern New Jersey learned how quickly an unprotected basement can take on water.

This is why we recommend a system and not a patch. A drain along the wall, a sump pit and a pump with a backup protect the basement during the storms that matter most.

What we do and what we refer out

Our work centers on the plumbing side. We install interior drainage that connects to a sump pump, set pumps and discharge lines, and install backwater valves. Exterior excavation, gutter work and masonry repair are separate trades, and we are open about the line between them.

Interior drainage and the sump connection

Many basement leaks are handled with a perimeter drain along the footing that carries water to a sump pit. The pump then sends it away from the house. The drain only works if the pit and pump are sized and maintained, so we look at the whole path of the water.

Cracks, window wells and a wall that stays damp each need a different fix. Sealing the inside of a wall without managing the water outside can trap moisture and fail within a season.

What we check on a first visit

A walk through the basement with a plan in hand turns a wet floor into a list of causes. You then choose what to fix first.

  • Gutters, downspouts and grading near the foundation.
  • Wall and floor cracks, and where moisture shows.
  • The sump pit, pump, check valve and discharge.
  • Floor drains and the main cleanout.
  • Signs of past water such as efflorescence or stained boxes.

Codes and standards behind the work

Drains and sump discharge must follow the International Plumbing Code as adopted under the New Jersey Uniform Construction Code, and any new pump circuit should be on a GFCI protected outlet as the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70) requires.
